You mention that you finally took care of your estate planning. Your daughter looks relieved. Your brother says he is glad you got it done. Then everyone moves on, and nobody asks where the plan is or how they would find the person who helped you create it.

An estate planning conversation can begin with something simple: “If you need to help me, here is where to start.” It can also open the door to preparing your children to understand and care for your family’s resources.

That conversation matters if you are in the hospital and unable to speak for yourself, or after you die. If I helped you create your plan, the people you have given me permission to speak with can call my office. I can explain the plan, answer questions about their roles, and help them understand the wishes you recorded. What I can share depends on your permissions, the person’s role, and applicable law.

With holiday visits approaching, you may have a chance to share that starting point in person. The goal is not a family meeting about every decision. It is to connect the people you trust with the guidance they may need, while you can explain what matters to you.
## Knowing You Have a Plan Is Not the Same as Knowing How to Find it

Imagine your family looking for one important contact. One person remembers a lawyer’s first name. Another thinks the folder is in a desk drawer. Someone finds an old copy but cannot tell whether it is the current one.

You can prevent some of that uncertainty without handing out the entire plan. **Start with my office’s name and contact information and a clear description of where your current records are kept.** Explain how the appropriate person would obtain them.

That last point matters. “It is in the safe” is not much help if nobody knows which safe, where the access instructions are, or whom to ask. A storage location and a workable access process are different things.

If your records are digital, explain where the instructions for authorized access are kept. Keep account passwords, security codes, and sensitive identification numbers in secure storage rather than a family group text. Give each person only the access appropriate to their role.

You do not need to create another complicated filing system. A brief, dated guide can point the right people toward the current information and the professional who can explain it.

**The bottom line:** Give your family a reliable starting point, not a scavenger hunt.
## Prepare the People Who Will Carry This Forward

You may worry that telling your children how much money you have will take away their motivation. But keeping family wealth a mystery gives them little opportunity to learn how to care for it. **Consider sharing what you have alongside the values, choices, and responsibilities that come with it.**

That preparation can begin while you are here to answer questions. Talk about what your resources make possible, invite questions from your children or whoever will help carry this forward, and discuss how they can learn to use those resources with purpose. The goal is preparation, not a promise about what anyone will inherit.

Alongside that conversation, share the practical starting points:

- My office’s name and current contact information.
- Where the appropriate person can find your current plan and access instructions.
- Whom to contact first if they cannot reach you.
- Which questions should come back to you or me rather than be guessed at.

Tell me which family members or other trusted people I may speak with about your plan, and what information I may share with each person. Your daughter having my number only helps if I am allowed to speak with her, so we clarify and document those permissions as part of planning.

Match the conversation to your children’s ages and readiness. Sharing information does not mean giving everyone the same access. You can still keep some decisions private. Your own needs, future care, and changing circumstances belong in the discussion, too.

If you are unsure how to begin with your children, bring that question to our planning conversation.

**The bottom line:** Help your children understand the purpose of your wealth, as well as where to find help.
## An Estate Planning Conversation Does Not Give Someone Authority

Telling your daughter where your records are does not, by itself, authorize her to manage your money. Neither does adding her to an email with your advisor. **Information helps someone get oriented; legal authority determines what they can do.**

A financial power of attorney is one tool for appointing someone to act on your behalf. Its scope and when it can be used depend on the document and applicable law. Being your spouse, child, or most organized relative is not a substitute for checking the actual arrangement.

Medical decisions need their own arrangements. A health care power of attorney can name a decision-maker, while a HIPAA authorization can permit sharing health information. Their scope, when they apply, and state-law rules matter.

The same distinction matters with a brokerage account’s trusted contact. That person may help the institution reach someone when there is a concern, but the designation alone does not give them authority to trade or withdraw money.

You do not need to teach your family every rule. You can say, “Knowing where this is does not mean you should start moving money. Call my office so you understand the next step.”

If a family member expects one role and your plan assigns another, address the mismatch privately. Ask questions before changing anything. The person you love most may not be the person best positioned to carry out a particular responsibility.

**The bottom line:** Make it clear who has information, who may act, and where to ask when those are not the same person.
## Choose a Small Conversation, Not a Holiday Ambush

A crowded dinner table is not the only opportunity to talk. A walk, a quiet coffee, or a short call after the visit may work better. Ask whether the person has a few minutes rather than announcing a serious discussion in front of everyone.

Keep the first conversation focused on a useful starting point. The person should leave knowing the first call to make and which questions to bring back to you. You can make time for a fuller discussion instead of trying to cover everything at once.

Ask them to tell you what they understood. “If you needed to help me, where would you start?” is more useful than “Does that all make sense?” You may discover that your clear explanation still left them picturing the wrong folder or the wrong person.

If there is family tension, private conversations can keep one relative from having to relay sensitive instructions to everyone else. Decide what each person needs to know and consider where professional guidance would help.

If you do not yet have a plan, the holiday conversation can still be useful. Tell your family you are beginning the process and ask about willingness to help, without presenting an informal promise as a legal appointment. **The bottom line:** A short conversation with a clear purpose can accomplish more than a surprise meeting about everything.
